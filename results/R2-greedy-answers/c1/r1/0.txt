For millennia, the coastline has been a place of both opportunity and peril. Ships navigating the dark waters near landmasses faced the constant threat of running aground on hidden rocks or shallow reefs. To mitigate this danger, humanity developed one of its most enduring and vital navigational aids: the lighthouse. The history of these structures is not merely a chronicle of architectural evolution but also a testament to the advancement of optical science, particularly the development of lenses that transformed simple fires into powerful, directed beams of light.

The earliest precursors to modern lighthouses were simple bonfires lit on high cliffs or headlands. Ancient civilizations, including the Greeks and Romans, used these fires to guide sailors, but they were inefficient and often unreliable. The most famous example from antiquity was the Pharos of Alexandria, built in the third century BC.
